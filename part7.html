01

The problem: attention does not scale, and everyone knew it

Softmax attention has two costs, and by 2025 both had become binding for agentic workloads.

Compute: O(n²)

Every token attends to every previous token. Doubling the context quadruples the prefill work. Reading a 256K-token repository costs sixteen times what reading a 64K subset costs — and prefill is where an agent spends most of its FLOPs.

Memory: O(n) per sequence, forever

The KV cache grows linearly and is never released while the sequence lives. It is per-sequence, so it caps concurrency directly. GQA cut the constant; it did not change the growth.

The workload that broke the compromise is the coding agent: a 200K-token repository context, re-prefilled or cached across dozens of turns, with dozens of concurrent sessions. Under full attention that is either impossibly slow or impossibly expensive, and usually both.

Why the obvious fixes were not enough

  • Sparse attention (windowed, strided, learned) drops interactions the model may need. It works well until the task requires exactly the interaction you dropped — and retrieval tasks are precisely that.
  • Pure linear attention / SSMs (Mamba and relatives) are O(n) and have a constant-size state. But a fixed-size state cannot hold arbitrary detail: they are measurably weaker at in-context recall, which is the one thing long context is for.
  • Sliding-window hybrids help, but still keep quadratic behaviour in the global layers.
The insight

You do not need every layer to be capable of arbitrary retrieval — you need some layers to be. Qwen3-Next keeps one full-attention layer in every four and makes the other three a linear recurrence. Retrieval capability is preserved by the 25%; cost is dominated by the 75%.

02

Gated DeltaNet — a recurrence that can actually retrieve

Gated DeltaNet comes from the 2024 paper Gated Delta Networks: Improving Mamba2 with Delta Rule. It is a linear-attention layer whose state is a fixed-size matrix memory, updated per token by two mechanisms that pull in opposite directions.

Why the delta rule is the difference

A plain gated recurrence (Mamba-style) accumulates: every token adds to the state, and old associations are diluted rather than replaced. The delta rule makes the update an error correction, so writing a new value for a key genuinely overwrites the old one. That is what turns a fixed-size state into something resembling an associative memory — and it is why Gated DeltaNet holds up on in-context recall where earlier linear-attention designs collapsed.

Cost, per token, is constant: the state is a fixed-size matrix regardless of sequence length. There is no KV cache in these layers — only the recurrent state.

03

The 3:1 hybrid stack

Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B is 48 layers arranged as twelve identical blocks. The model card states the layout precisely: 12 × (3 × (Gated DeltaNet → MoE) → 1 × (Gated Attention → MoE)).

Two details in the attention layer are worth noting. Partial RoPE — rotary embeddings applied to only 64 of the 256 head dimensions — leaves most of the head space position-agnostic, which improves extrapolation to contexts far beyond the trained length. And the output gate suppresses attention sinks and massive activations, the pathologies that make very long-context attention numerically awkward and hard to quantise.

04

Extreme sparsity — 512 experts, 10 active

Qwen3's MoE used 128 experts with 8 active. Qwen3-Next quadruples the expert count and adds back a shared expert: 512 routed experts, 10 activated per token, plus 1 always-on shared expert, with an expert intermediate dimension of just 512.

Why more, narrower experts

  • Routing resolution. Selecting 10 of 512 gives astronomically more distinct expert combinations than 8 of 128, so specialisation can be much finer-grained.
  • Better parameter-to-FLOP ratio. Narrow experts mean you can hold far more total knowledge for the same per-token compute — 80B of capacity for 3B of work.
  • The shared expert returns. With 512 narrow experts, common knowledge would otherwise be duplicated across hundreds of them. One always-on shared expert holds it once. This is the Qwen1.5-MoE idea, dropped in Qwen3, reinstated when the expert count grew.
The cost of extreme sparsity

All 512 experts must be resident in memory, so this is still an 80B-weight model — roughly 160GB at BF16. And routing 10 of 512 makes expert parallelism nearly mandatory at scale: with tensor parallelism alone, every GPU holds a slice of every expert and the all-to-all traffic dominates. --enable-expert-parallel is not a tuning flag here, it is the intended topology.

05

The stability toolkit

Training a 48-layer hybrid with 512 experts at 3B active is numerically hostile. Qwen3-Next carries a set of explicit stabilisation measures, and they are worth knowing because they explain some of the model's quantisation behaviour too.

Zero-centred RMSNorm with weight decay

Standard RMSNorm learns a per-channel gain that can drift to extreme values, producing activation outliers. Centring the gain at zero and applying weight decay to it keeps the scale bounded. Fewer outliers means more stable training and a much friendlier target for FP8 and INT4 quantisation.

Attention output gating

A learned gate on the attention output suppresses attention sinks — the degenerate pattern where heads dump probability mass on the first token — and the massive activations that accompany them.

Global-batch load balancing

Carried over from Qwen3. With 512 experts, router collapse is a much bigger risk: if traffic concentrates on 30 experts, 94% of the parameter budget is dead weight.

Normalised router initialisation

Initialising the router so that all experts start unbiased prevents the rich-get-richer dynamic in the first few thousand steps, when an accidental early preference can become permanent.

Why this matters downstream

Activation outliers are the main reason post-training quantisation degrades quality: a single channel with a huge range forces a coarse scale on everything sharing that tensor. A model architected to avoid outliers quantises better almost for free — which is a large part of why the 2026 Qwen models ship credible FP8 checkpoints as a matter of course.

06

Multi-token prediction

Qwen3-Next was trained with an MTP objective: alongside the usual next-token head, additional heads predict tokens two and three positions ahead. This helps pre-training slightly, and helps inference a great deal.

Turning MTP on in vLLM — no application changes required
vllm serve Qwen/Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct \
  --port 8000 --tensor-parallel-size 4 --max-model-len 262144 \
  --speculative-config '{"method":"qwen3_next_mtp","num_speculative_tokens":2}'

# SGLang uses its NEXTN speculative path for the same mechanism
python -m sglang.launch_server --model-path Qwen/Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct \
  --port 30000 --tp-size 4 --context-length 262144 --mem-fraction-static 0.8 \
  --speculative-algo NEXTN --speculative-num-steps 3 \
  --speculative-eagle-topk 1 --speculative-num-draft-tokens 4
Tuning rule

Enable MTP for latency-sensitive, low-concurrency workloads — an interactive coding agent, a voice front-end. Disable it for throughput-oriented batch workloads, where the verification overhead eats into aggregate tokens per second. The vLLM recipes also suggest disabling prefix caching alongside MTP for the lowest-latency configuration.

07

What vLLM had to build

A hybrid model is genuinely awkward to serve, because half the layers have a KV cache that grows with the sequence and half have a fixed-size recurrent state. vLLM's paged-memory design assumes the former. Three pieces of engineering closed the gap, and they shipped alongside the model.

Hybrid KV cache manager

Full-attention layers use paged KV blocks; linear layers hold a constant-size state. The manager auto-tunes the logical block size of the attention layers so their physical memory footprint aligns with the linear layers' state — keeping one uniform allocator and avoiding fragmentation.

Flash Linear Attention kernels

Gated DeltaNet needs chunked-parallel scan kernels that do not exist in cuDNN or FlashAttention. vLLM adopted the Triton kernels from the Flash Linear Attention project rather than writing its own.

Full CUDA graph mode by default

Triton kernels carry noticeable per-launch CPU overhead, and a hybrid layer stack means many more launches per forward pass. Capturing the whole decode step as a CUDA graph removes that overhead — essential for decode-only batches where the GPU work per launch is tiny.

Serving Qwen3-Next
# At release this needed a nightly wheel; it is in stable vLLM now.
vllm serve Qwen/Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct --tensor-parallel-size 4

# Thinking variant — add the reasoning parser
vllm serve Qwen/Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking \
  --tensor-parallel-size 4 --reasoning-parser qwen3 \
  --max-model-len 262144

# High-sparsity MoE: prefer expert parallelism over pure tensor parallelism
vllm serve Qwen/Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct \
  --tensor-parallel-size 4 --enable-expert-parallel

The practical lesson for anyone evaluating a novel architecture: check serving support before you check benchmarks. A model that needs a nightly build, an unmerged kernel and a custom memory manager is not deployable, however good its scores are. Qwen3-Next was usable in vLLM within a day of release because the Qwen team and the vLLM team did the work in parallel — and that co-development is now the normal pattern for the family.

08

The numbers that justified the pivot

Where the throughput comes from

  • Prefill — 36 of 48 layers are linear, so prefill work grows nearly linearly with context rather than quadratically. This is the dominant win for repository-scale prompts.
  • Decode — only 3B parameters are read per token, and only 12 layers consult a KV cache, so per-step memory traffic is small and roughly context-independent.
  • Concurrency — a 75% smaller cached-layer count means far more sequences fit in the same HBM, which multiplies aggregate throughput on top of the per-request gains.
  • MTP — a further multiplier on top, at low concurrency.

Why a “preview” became the mainline

Qwen3-Next was framed as an experiment in next-generation architecture. Five months later, the design is the entire product line.

What to take from this deck

If you understand the 3:1 hybrid, the 512-expert MoE and MTP, you understand the architecture of every current Qwen model. Parts 09 and 10 are largely about what changes around that core: native multimodality, more languages, better post-training, and scale.
